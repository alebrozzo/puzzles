# The Last Dinner at Vale House — story skeleton

This is a complete working case, including the solution. It develops the existing opening tables without replacing them. Names remain placeholders. For this draft, Dave is the **doctor**.

## Case premise

Walter Vale, owner of Vale House, has summoned four people who do not know one another. Each invitation says: **“I have decided to settle our unfinished business. Come to dinner at seven.”**

Vale intends to give each guest a sealed packet containing material he has kept for years and to report one of those matters to the police the next morning. Before dinner he speaks privately to every guest. Shortly after dinner begins, a deliberate power cut plunges the house into darkness. When the lights come back, Vale is found dead in his study.

The murderer is **Carl, Vale’s nephew**. Years earlier, Vale helped Carl provide a false alibi after Carl caused a hit-and-run collision. Vale has now received a telegram from the person who supplied that alibi, withdrawing it. Carl kills Vale to prevent the telegram and Vale’s statement from reaching the police.

Vale dies instantly after Carl strikes him with a small bronze horse statuette from the gallery. Carl cuts the power, reaches the study through the service passage, takes the packet addressed to him, kills Vale, wraps the statuette in his dinner napkin, and hides it in the lining of his coat.

## What the reader should know at the end

- Vale was alive at 8:10, carrying four sealed packets into his study.
- The blackout began at 8:12 because somebody removed the basement fuse; it was not caused by the storm.
- Vale was killed between 8:12 and 8:16 by the bronze horse statuette.
- Alice, Bob, and Dave each have independently supported accounts for that interval.
- Carl’s notebook cannot support his claim that he stayed in the drawing room.
- Carl had both a powerful reason to stop Vale and the opportunity to cause the blackout, use the service passage, and reach the study.
- Bronze dust, blue napkin fibres, and red sealing wax tie Carl to the weapon and to the packet he claimed not to have opened.

## Puzzle-by-puzzle story

| # | Puzzle and possible form | What the reader does | Exact discovery and story movement |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The body is discovered** — four-set logic grid | Match each guest to their relationship with Vale, their location when the body was discovered, and what they said they were doing. | Introduces Alice (business partner), Bob (neighbor), Carl (nephew), and Dave (doctor). It records their first accounts: Alice says she was telephoning in the library; Bob was examining a painting in the conservatory; Carl was writing in a notebook in the drawing room; Dave was looking for his coat in the gallery. These are accounts at the time of discovery, not yet murder alibis. |
| 2 | **The dinner table** — visual place-setting match | Match guests to places at the round dining table using overlapping details: glass garnish, napkin fold, missing spoon or bread, and seat. | Establishes the physical layout of dinner. Carl’s place is the south seat and has the only folded napkin; Alice and Bob both have lemon in their glasses, so it cannot be solved from one detail. The reader learns that Vale left the table at 8:10 to fetch four sealed packets from his study. The folded napkin becomes important later because it is used to wrap the bronze statue. |
| 3 | **Reasons to fear Vale** — four-set logic grid | Match each guest to a possible motive, an item found by the detective, and the place it was found. | Every guest has a serious reason to want Vale silent. Alice fears a decades-old accident report; Bob fears an undisclosed debt; Carl fears the exposure of a false alibi; Dave fears a family identity record becoming public. The detective has motives, but no reason yet to privilege one over another. |
| 4 | **Vale’s private meetings** — ordering/timeline puzzle | Use guest accounts and household records to place Vale’s four private conversations in order. | The meetings occur in this order: Alice, Bob, Carl, Dave. Carl’s meeting is between 7:50 and 8:00. In it, Vale tells Carl that the person who gave his false alibi has withdrawn it and that Vale will send the telegram and his own statement to the police in the morning. Dave speaks with Vale last and sees him alive until 8:10. The reader now knows why Carl’s motive is urgent, but still has no proof he acted on it. |
| 5 | **The study before and after** — spot-the-difference scene puzzle | Compare the study as photographed before dinner with the study after the body is found. | Three meaningful changes are found: the bronze horse statuette has vanished from the gallery display shelf; the west study door, which opens onto a service passage, stands open; and the sealed blue packet marked for Carl is missing from the set of four packets. The room now supplies a plausible weapon, route, and stolen item, but does not identify who used them. |
| 6 | **The blackout route** — house-plan path puzzle | Trace the service routes from the dining room, drawing room, gallery, conservatory, library, basement fuse box, and study. Follow the wet marks and one-way swing doors. | The detective proves the blackout was deliberate: the basement fuse was removed at 8:12. The route from the drawing room to the fuse box and then through the service passage to the study can be completed in under four minutes without crossing the main hall. The same route also passes the gallery shelf where the horse stood. The storm is no longer an explanation for the darkness. |
| 7 | **What killed Vale?** — evidence-matching grid | Match marks on Vale’s injury, traces in the study, and objects from the gallery. | The crescent-shaped cut and tiny bronze fragments show that the missing bronze horse, rather than a knife or one of the larger display objects, killed Vale. The doctor estimates death at 8:12–8:16 and says the blow was immediately fatal. This is the first time the detective can define the true murder window and weapon. |
| 8 | **Interview: Alice** — telephone-log deduction | Reconstruct Alice’s call from the library telephone, the exchange log, and the recipient’s notes. | Alice spoke to a newspaper editor from 8:11 to 8:19 about correcting the old accident story. The call is independently confirmed, so she cannot have removed the fuse, collected the statue, and delivered the fatal blow in the 8:12–8:16 window. She also reports seeing Vale take all four blue packets toward the study at 8:10. Alice is cleared of the murder, though her motive was real. |
| 9 | **Interview: Bob** — conservatory observation puzzle | Sort the conservatory observations by when they happened: a lightning flash, the greenhouse heater’s warning light, the sound of the basement door, and the return of electricity. | Bob was in the conservatory throughout the fatal interval, repairing the heater’s warning light; its timed maintenance strip and his dirty gloves support this. During the lightning flash at 8:13, he saw light briefly move behind the service-passage fanlight, so he knows someone used that passage. He heard the basement door shut just before it. Bob cannot name the person, but the route is now confirmed by a witness. |
| 10 | **Interview: Dave** — records and medical observations | Match Dave’s short call with his clinic’s answering service and use his observations of the body. | Dave took a clinic call from the gallery phone from 8:12 to 8:17; the service record and the caller’s message agree. He is therefore cleared from the murder window. He adds that the killer would have needed to leave the study almost immediately after striking Vale: there was no attempt to treat, move, or stage the body. Alice, Bob, and Dave now have supported accounts; Carl does not. |
| 11 | **Interview: Carl** — notebook and paper-trace comparison | Compare Carl’s claimed notebook entry with the room conditions, the packet paper, and the table setting. | Carl says he spent the blackout in the drawing room writing. The page he claims to have written on is dry despite the ink used taking several minutes to set, and its indentation comes from an older page rather than a new entry. More importantly, a strip of blue fibres in the notebook binding matches his folded dinner napkin, and red sealing wax from Vale’s packet is caught in the same fold. Carl opened his packet and used the napkin after leaving the table; the notebook was created as a cover. The detective has a decisive contradiction, but still needs the weapon. |
| 12 | **Final case file: who killed Vale?** — evidence-synthesis deduction | Use the case-file facts to assign the fuse removal, service-passage journey, weapon, stolen packet, and false alibi to the one person who can account for all five. | Carl is the only answer. A search of his coat, authorized after the deduction, finds the bronze horse in the torn lining, wrapped in the blue folded napkin. Carl removed the fuse, took the statue from the gallery, entered the study through the west door, killed Vale, stole and opened the packet about his false alibi, then returned through the dark passage before the lights came back. The final reconstruction gives culprit, motive, method, route, and the physical evidence that proves it. |

## Case-file facts for the finale

The final page can provide blank fields for readers to record only these facts:

1. Vale was last seen alive at **8:10**.
2. The blackout began at **8:12** and was caused by a removed fuse.
3. Death occurred between **8:12 and 8:16**.
4. The murder weapon was the **bronze horse statuette**.
5. The killer used the **service passage** and took **Carl’s blue packet**.
6. Alice, Bob, and Dave each have a supported account for the fatal interval.
7. Carl’s notebook, napkin, packet wax, and coat lining connect him to the crime.

## Details deliberately left open

- Vale’s final name, the exact name of the old hit-and-run victim, and the precise contents of the other three packets.
- The tone and severity of the old accident and other secrets, so they can be adjusted for the desired age range.
- Exact puzzle clues, artwork, page count, and individual difficulty ratings.
- Whether the host’s name, house name, and title remain “Vale.”
