# Puzzle type catalogue

A reusable menu for planning puzzle books. The categories describe the main thinking skill a puzzle asks for; an individual puzzle can combine several. Choose a puzzle for the discovery or experience it should create, then pick a mechanic that supports it.

## Deduction and constraint puzzles

- **Logic grids:** Use clues to match items across several categories, such as people, places, and times. Good for layered deductions; vary the number of categories and clue complexity.
- **Ordering and scheduling:** Put events, people, or objects in an order or timetable from relative clues. Can be a compact deduction or a substantial multi-step puzzle.
- **Truth, lies, and statements:** Determine which statements are true, who is lying, or which claim is consistent with the facts. Depends on careful wording; best when the rules are explicit.
- **Grouping and classification:** Sort items into groups using shared properties or constraints. Useful for themes, collections, teams, and hidden categories.
- **Assignment and matching:** Pair items using clues, sometimes with fewer categories or a visual board than a full logic grid. Keep enough interacting clues to make the solve satisfying.
- **Cause-and-effect deduction:** Infer what happened, or which conditions produced an outcome, from a set of facts. Can reveal a story event without centering on suspects.

## Words and language

- **Word searches:** Find listed words in a letter field. Accessible and visual; can be made more purposeful by using found words as inputs to a second step.
- **Crosswords and cross-number clues:** Fill a grid from definitions or themed prompts. Clues can range from direct to cryptic; crossing answers provide support.
- **Anagrams and letter rearrangements:** Rearrange letters to form words or phrases. Use as a standalone solve or as an extraction step.
- **Hidden words and letter extraction:** Find a message in first/last letters, marked positions, or selected answers. State the selection rule clearly.
- **Word ladders and transformations:** Change one word into another through constrained edits. Works well for short, progressive challenges.
- **Codes based on language:** Use substitutions, shifts, transpositions, or symbol alphabets to decode text. Give enough examples or a key to make the intended system discoverable.
- **Definitions, riddles, and wordplay:** Reach an answer through meaning, ambiguity, or a compact verbal clue. Difficulty can vary greatly with cultural knowledge, so keep answers fair and grounded.
- **Acrostics and phrase assembly:** Combine initial, final, or otherwise selected letters from answers to reveal a word or instruction.

## Numbers and patterns

- **Arithmetic challenges:** Use operations and constraints to reach a target or fill missing values. Avoid requiring obscure calculation tricks unless taught in the book.
- **Number sequences:** Infer a rule and continue or repair a sequence. Make the intended rule sufficiently constrained; many sequences admit multiple valid rules.
- **Numeric logic grids:** Apply deduction to numbers, quantities, or measurements. Can combine arithmetic with matching or scheduling.
- **Equations and missing symbols:** Insert values or operations to make statements valid. Difficulty can come from the number of unknowns and permitted operations.
- **Visual or symbolic sequences:** Infer what changes across a series of shapes, icons, or symbols. Useful when a book needs variety beyond text-heavy puzzles.
- **Data interpretation:** Read a chart, table, tally, or set of measurements to answer a question or uncover a conclusion. Can feel grounded in the book's world.

## Visual, spatial, and observation puzzles

- **Spot the difference / anomaly finding:** Compare images or records and identify changes or inconsistencies. Keep the target differences unambiguous at print size.
- **Visual matching:** Match silhouettes, patterns, objects, or details to their counterparts. Can be a quick entry puzzle or a clue-rich deduction.
- **Mazes and route finding:** Find a path through a map or network, sometimes under rules such as one-way passages or limited moves.
- **Maps and network puzzles:** Use a map, floor plan, or connected-node diagram to infer distance, access, or a route. Can support many settings beyond mysteries.
- **Tangrams and shape assembly:** Arrange pieces to make a target form or satisfy a spatial condition. Consider whether readers can mark, cut, or mentally manipulate pieces.
- **Folding, rotation, and orientation:** Infer how a shape or object changes when turned, reflected, or folded. Diagrams should make viewpoint and direction clear.
- **Line tracing and path logic:** Trace paths through a diagram subject to crossing, region, or connection rules. Often easy to explain and visually distinctive.
- **Picture or scene interpretation:** Find meaningful details in an illustration, diagram, or layout. Make sure the answer depends on visible evidence rather than guesswork.

## Codes, symbols, and information handling

- **Cipher decoding:** Translate an encoded message using a provided key or a pattern readers can infer.
- **Symbol substitution:** Map icons or marks to letters, numbers, or concepts. The key may be given, partially given, or deduced.
- **Transposition and rearrangement:** Recover information by restoring a shuffled order, grid, or sequence.
- **Filtering and extraction:** Select the relevant details from a larger set, then read or compute the result. This can connect separate puzzles into a chain.
- **Error correction:** Find and repair an incorrect entry using consistency rules. Useful for making readers inspect records critically.
- **Information comparison:** Compare versions, reports, inventories, or messages to determine what changed or which account fits.

## Strategy and experimentation

- **Move-sequence puzzles:** Reach a goal by applying legal moves, such as sliding tiles or rearranging objects. Solutions can reward planning ahead.
- **Resource and trade-off puzzles:** Achieve a goal with limited turns, objects, time, or capacity. State all limits and success conditions.
- **Game and strategy puzzles:** Find a winning move or strategy under a defined set of rules. Explain whose turn it is and what counts as winning.
- **Trial-and-deduction puzzles:** Test possibilities and use outcomes to narrow them down. Works best when tests are systematic and the number of possibilities is manageable.
- **Optimization puzzles:** Find the shortest, cheapest, or most efficient solution. Specify whether one optimum or any valid solution is wanted.

## Story, interpretation, and open-ended forms

- **Riddles and lateral-thinking scenarios:** Infer an explanation from a short situation. These can rely on a surprising assumption, so provide enough constraints to keep the answer fair.
- **Sequence reconstruction:** Put events, pages, messages, or images in a plausible order from internal evidence.
- **Deduce the hidden rule:** Identify the rule that separates valid from invalid examples, then apply it to new cases.
- **Evidence synthesis:** Combine facts from multiple sources or earlier puzzles to reach a supported conclusion. Good for finales; record or repeat only the information that the finale truly needs.
- **Interpretive puzzles:** Explain a pattern, image, or text in a way supported by evidence. Decide whether the book expects one answer or accepts a range.
- **Creative challenges:** Invite readers to draw, invent, write, or design under constraints. These are engaging but may need sample answers or criteria instead of a single answer key.

## Choosing and balancing puzzle types

When planning a book, track more than the puzzle label:

- **Primary skill:** deduction, vocabulary, arithmetic, spatial reasoning, observation, or strategy.
- **Format:** grid, prose, illustration, table, map, code, or hands-on layout.
- **Answer shape:** one word, one value, a complete arrangement, a path, or an explanation.
- **Difficulty levers:** number of items, number of steps, clue directness, required precision, and whether earlier answers are needed.
- **Accessibility:** reading load, visual detail at print size, arithmetic burden, cultural knowledge, and whether instructions can be understood without special materials.
- **Story role:** introduce a setting, reveal a fact, offer a change of pace, connect earlier information, or resolve a question.

Variety comes from changing the thinking skill and presentation, not just changing the story wrapper. A book can also use a familiar mechanic at a new difficulty or combine two mechanics when the combination earns its space.
